\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A graph index and its search-budget policy jointly determine the quality and cost of approximate nearest-neighbor search (ANNS). Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graphs and Vamana make this tradeoff central to efficient vector retrieval~\cite{malkov2020hnsw,subramanya2019diskann}. Index maintenance changes the execution environment of that policy. Construction order and randomization can alter traversal even when vectors and queries are unchanged; data refresh can also change nearest-neighbor truth. A budget retained across rebuilds is therefore a portability decision, not inert configuration.

Portability matters at the query-risk level. We measure the probability of $\rec@10<0.95$: because recall is discrete, one missing neighbor is a failure. A 5\% risk limit asks that at least 95\% of queries recover all ten neighbors. This requirement differs from mean recall of 0.95 and from the expected missed-neighbor fraction. It also makes target difficulty visible: a transferred action can fail because the target itself contains unresolved queries or because the source action adds loss beyond the target-specific reference.

We build the evidence in three stages. First, on unchanged SIFT-100K and Arxiv-Nomic-100K collections, we transfer each query's retrospective first-passing action across hnswlib and Faiss rebuilds. Additional target failure ranges from 17.17 to 23.60 percentage points across all four implementation--dataset blocks. Second, after a 5\% data refresh, we replay an executable recurring-query policy without changing its source information. Its risk rises from 3.77\% to 6.69\% on SIFT and from 3.19\% to 7.24\% on Arxiv-Nomic. Third, we freeze source-derived recovery candidates and reserve fresh queries for target certification and evaluation. This stage qualifies all 1,104 Faiss decisions across the two 100K datasets and all 56 hnswlib decisions in a preregistered Deep1M replication, while retaining positive mean and tail results.

\emph{Index-Conditioned Budget Audit} (ICBA) organizes these observations as a deployment decision. It fixes the permitted observations and failure event, separates candidate construction from target certification and evaluation, includes fallback in the completed outcome, and prices information acquisition. The resulting contract prevents response evidence, statistical qualification, serving-work savings, and lifecycle value from being treated as the same quantity.

We instantiate two recovery routes within that contract. \emph{Target-calibrated pooling} (TCP) pools historical budgets for recurring profiled queries and selects one target shift before independent certification. Under a joint 5\% confidence-error allocation, it retains mean-distance reductions of 44.39\% on SIFT and 29.86\% on Arxiv-Nomic. The fixed-slack route removes target selection: it freezes a one-rung source-derived candidate, then uses fresh target queries only to certify and evaluate that candidate. Its source-only stage exposes an implementation boundary on the 100K grids; its target-certified Faiss and Deep1M replications establish recovery across implementations and scale. We make three contributions. First, a layered empirical study isolates graph-only response change, matched refresh-policy deterioration, and target-certified execution across hnswlib, Faiss, two data families, and 100K-to-1M scale. Second, ICBA supplies a reusable contract for observations, query roles, risk qualification, fallback, and cost; its fixed-target certificate states exactly what a target decision authorizes. Third, TCP and the fixed-slack route demonstrate complementary recovery regimes. TCP serves recurring profiles with target selection, while fixed slack uses a frozen source candidate followed by target certification. Their positive and endpoint-clipped cases identify where each route retains useful work and when its information cost amortizes. DARTH, Ada-ef, and Vamana analyses extend the scope without forming a common leaderboard.

The systems implication is concrete: version a search-budget policy with the index, workload, and calibration record. Reuse requires target evidence, and the appropriate recovery route depends on which observations are available and how long the resulting policy will serve.
